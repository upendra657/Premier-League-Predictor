Interview Prep — Premier League Decision Engine

The questions you're ready for, and the four that actually bite. Every number here is reproducible from the repo.

Opening — the 90-second version

"I rebuilt a match-outcome classifier into a decision engine. The reframing was the point: instead of asking who wins, which bookmakers already answer better than I can, I asked where is the market wrong, by how much, and how much should you stake on it."

"That changes what you optimise. Accuracy becomes irrelevant — I evaluate on Brier score, log loss and calibration error, benchmarked against the de-vigged bookmaker line. And it forces a financial test rather than a statistical one: I backtest the probabilities against 13,527 historical prices with fractional Kelly staking."

"The headline is +7.2% ROI over twelve held-out seasons. The more useful finding is that when I refit the selection rule out-of-sample it drops to +1.5% and stops being significant — so what I actually built is a rigorous demonstration that this market is close to efficient, plus the machinery to detect it if it ever isn't."

The four findings — and the follow-up after the answer

Your model loses to the market on Brier score. Isn't that a failed model?

Brier conflates two properties. The market discriminates better — it has team news, lineups and money flow I don't. But calibration and discrimination are different things, and Kelly staking depends on the second one: it takes the probability at face value, so a well-calibrated 30% is worth more than a sharper but overconfident 30%.

They will then ask

"Your ECE is 0.0093 against the market's 0.0105. That's a 0.0012 gap. Is it significant?"

Don't oversell it. "I didn't put a confidence interval on ECE, and I wouldn't lean on a gap that size. The calibration claim is directional. The load-bearing evidence is the backtest — that's a financial test of whether the probabilities support decisions, and it doesn't depend on winning a metric by 0.0012."

Why did calibration help Random Forest but not XGBoost?

Random Forest probabilities are vote proportions, fitted against no probabilistic loss, so they cluster toward the middle. XGBoost trained on multi:softprob with mlogloss is directly optimising a strictly proper scoring rule — calibration is already in the objective. Isotonic had nothing to correct and added fold variance. It's a targeted remedy for a specific defect, not a free improvement.

The question that can end badly

"So you served the better-calibrated model?" — No, and this is the sharpest thing in the project. Random Forest + isotonic wins on Brier, RPS and ECE. It also backtests at −18.5%. The model actually served is uncalibrated XGBoost, which scores worse on three of four metrics and returns +1.5%.

Volunteer this — don't wait to be caught

"Aggregate calibration is measured over all predictions. Betting only samples the tail where the model disagrees with the market. Isotonic regression is a monotone step function fitted on sparse tail bins, so it flattens exactly the disagreements the strategy trades on — 279 qualifying bets against 877. A model can be well calibrated on average and useless precisely where you act on it. So the backtest selected the served model, not the scoring rule — which is the whole argument for having a decision layer at all."

You quote +7.2% ROI but also +1.5%. Which is real?

+1.5% is the honest expectation, and its confidence interval straddles zero. The +7.2% uses a selection band chosen with knowledge of the full sample. Refit that band on 2013/14–2018/19, apply it blind to the next six seasons, and it falls to +1.5%. I report both because the gap is the finding: a marginal, unstable edge in a near-efficient market.

They will then ask

"Then why lead with the number you don't believe?"

"Because it's the correct answer to a different question. +7.2% is what the strategy returned over the period; +1.5% is what I'd forecast forward. The first is a measurement, the second is a prediction. I'd never quote the first without the second — which is why they're adjacent in every artifact."

Why a band on relative edge instead of a threshold?

Because the return profile is non-monotonic in edge, and I measured it. Below 10% relative edge the model returns −1% to −4% — noise that can't clear the overround. Between 10% and 40%: +10.2% and +2.6%. Above 40%: −4.7%, longshots where the model is simply wrong. A one-sided threshold treats the profitable middle and the loss-making tail as one signal. That's why naive positive-EV systems lose money.

They will then ask

"Six buckets on 13,527 rows — how do you know that shape isn't overfitting?"

"I don't, fully, and that's exactly what the holdout test is for. The band was refit on the first six seasons and applied blind — the shape survived directionally but the magnitude collapsed. So I'd claim the non-monotonicity is real and the specific boundaries are not. If I were deploying this I'd re-fit the band every season and expect it to move."

The four that actually bite prepare these

Your best-odds execution takes the maximum price across every bookmaker. Could you actually have got those prices?

This is the strongest objection to the whole project, and it's practical rather than statistical. Take it head-on.

"Partly. It assumes accounts at every book in the sample, catching the best price before it moves, and — the real problem — not being limited. Bettors who consistently beat the closing line get stake-restricted or closed within months. So the +7.2% is an upper bound on what's executable, and the single-book figure of +3.2% is the more realistic ceiling. I report both for exactly that reason."

Then turn it into the finding

"It's also why I treat execution as a first-class result rather than an implementation detail. Single-book overround is 4.49%; best-of-book is 0.35%, over the same 7,570 fixtures. That four-point swing is larger than every modelling gain in the project combined. Where you transact matters more than what you predict — which is a fairly general lesson about deploying models into markets."

Maximum drawdown is −47%. Would anyone actually have kept betting?

"Probably not, and that's a real limitation of backtests — they assume a bettor with no emotions and no liquidity constraints. A −47% drawdown on a £1,000 bankroll is £470 gone, and 2021/22 lost 11.2% across a full season."

"It's also why I used quarter-Kelly and a 10% daily exposure cap rather than full Kelly, which is growth-optimal but has brutal variance. Full Kelly on probabilities estimated with error is close to a guaranteed ruin. The sizing is deliberately conservative relative to the theory."

You dropped 457 of 9,380 fixtures. Does that bias anything?

"Those are burn-in: the first season, where rolling features and Elo have no history. They're excluded from training and evaluation, not silently imputed. It biases toward slightly optimistic feature quality — every scored fixture has a full history behind it, which a live system wouldn't have for a promoted club's first matches."

"The API surfaces the same issue honestly: ask it about Burnley and the response carries as_of: 2024-05-19, because they were relegated. Stale features are labelled rather than served as current."

Walk me through how you'd know if this broke in production.

"Three layers. Data contracts fail loudly on upstream drift — odds-coverage floor, one-to-one join validation, and a date parser that cross-checks every fixture against its stated season and raises if more than 1% fall outside the window. That last one exists because opening the CSV in Excel silently rewrote ISO dates into ambiguous M/D/YY."

"Then calibration drift: ECE on a rolling window, alerting if the served model's stated confidence stops matching outcomes. And for the betting layer specifically I'd track closing-line value rather than ROI — whether I'm beating the price the market settles at. CLV reaches significance in hundreds of bets where ROI needs thousands, so it's the leading indicator."

Volunteer these before you're asked

  • The served model isn't the best-calibrated one. Get to it before they do — it demonstrates you understand your own result.
  • The holdout ROI isn't significant. p = 0.379.
  • xG is a Poisson proxy fitted on shots, corners and shots on target — not real shot-level xG from tracking data.
  • Rolling form isn't venue-split, so a team's last five matches alternate home and away and the feature blends two different contexts. Documented, not fixed.
  • No player availability data — probably the largest unmodelled signal, and a systematic reason the market beats the model.

Ask them

  • "When a model here is well calibrated but the downstream decisions still lose money — how does the team find that out?"
  • "What's the gap between your offline metric and the thing the business actually measures?"
  • "How much of the work is modelling versus the execution layer around it? I found execution dominated by a factor of five."
  • "How do you handle a result that's rigorous but negative? Is there room to report that something doesn't work?"
